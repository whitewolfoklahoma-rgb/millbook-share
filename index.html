Scale vs. tally — where the hidden margin lives

Every log saws out more lumber than Doyle says, and usually more than Scribner says. If you bill on the scale, that overrun is lumber you produced and gave away. This report totals it per job — and prices it — so you can decide whether to keep billing on scale, switch to tally, or raise the scale rate.

What MillBook does

A portable-sawmill book in four parts: the Log Calculator prices one log three ways (Doyle, Scribner Decimal C, International ¼″) so you see the spread before you quote; Jobs turns logs into priced job sheets; the Log Deck tracks whose logs are waiting, sawing, or done; and Scale vs. Tally grades each scale against the lumber you actually stacked.

The honest limits

The three scales are published formulas and tables (Settings → "How the scales work" shows the math), but every one is an estimate — Doyle famously under-runs small logs. How the scales work

Doyle — ((diameter − 4) ÷ 4)² × length. Simple, old, and famously stingy on small logs: it was built around thick slabs and a ¼″ kerf allowance that modern bandmills beat easily. Most custom sawyers who bill on Doyle know they're leaving lumber unbilled — this book shows exactly how much.

Scribner (Decimal C) — the US Forest Service standard, drawn from diagrams of 1″ boards. MillBook uses the published Decimal C table (diameters rounded to the nearest inch, volumes to the nearest 10 bf) scaled by length.

International ¼″ — the closest of the three to what a good mill actually saws. Computed the published way: the log is divided into 4-ft sections, each section scores 0.22D² − 0.71D with a ½″ taper allowance per section.

Your actual tally — the boards you really stacked, thickness × width × length ÷ 144 each — is the ground truth both scales are graded against. Enter it per log as you saw and the Scale vs. Tally report does the rest.
